Data-center growth is concentrating water demand in stressed basins, yet facility-level evidence is scarce and operators rarely disclose consumption. Recent work maps where these burdens fall nationally, but under a single accounting convention. We ask: how much of the apparent burden is set by the accounting choice itself?

We build a facility-level Scope 1/2/3 water estimator for 243 data-center buildings in Prince William County, Virginia - within the world's largest data-center region - from public records only: air permits, utility filings, transmission plans, and county GIS. Of these, 54 operate today (10.5 MGD); the rest are permitted or under construction, giving a full-buildout estimate of 49.6 MGD. Power, the model's root, follows an evidence ladder from permit-documented generator capacity to a floor-area regression, leave-one-out calibrated per building (90% coverage 0.86). We test the distribution against the only metered benchmark available - utility-reported 2023 data compiled by Virginia's legislative audit commission, which covers the study county and is thus partially, not fully, independent: all six published constraints hold and the shapes are indistinguishable (KS p = 0.09).

First, indirect water dominates (87%), and two standard conventions implicate different watersheds. Apportioning the grid's water among all users assigns over 40% of electricity-related water to a reservoir 80 km outside the host basin; asking what an added load causes assigns none, as the marginal generator is never nuclear. Neither is incorrect, but basin plans built on one do not transfer to the other. Second, inference is costly: floor-area-inferred power carries calibrated +/-60% intervals, while the tier that would resolve it - operator-disclosed load - is empty across all 243 buildings. Third, resolution governs stress: the basin holding most buildings reads 4% of mean annual flow but 23-34% of July. The regional authority's own assessment covers on-site use only.
