Data-center growth is concentrating water demand in stressed basins, yet facility-level evidence remains scarce. We reconstruct the water footprint of 243 data-center buildings in Prince William County, Virginia, using public records only, adapting the carbon-accounting scope framework to water: on-site cooling (Scope 1), electricity generation (Scope 2), and supply chain (Scope 3). Fifty-four buildings currently operate, using an estimated 10 million gallons per day (MGD); full buildout could reach roughly 50 MGD. We quantify uncertainty in inferred power demand and test our estimates against utility-reported 2023 data compiled by Virginia's legislative audit commission. We cannot reject the hypothesis that the two distributions arise from the same process (KS p = 0.09, n = 54; the test has limited power at this sample size), and the benchmark includes Prince William County, so it is not fully independent.

Electricity-related water accounts for 87% of the estimated footprint, on-site cooling for 4%, and supply chain for the remainder. Two accounting approaches point to different watersheds. Allocating grid water use across electricity consumers assigns more than 40% of electricity-related water to Lake Anna, 80 km outside the host basin. Asking instead what water use an added load causes assigns none to Lake Anna, because nuclear does not appear among reported marginal resources. Neither approach is inherently incorrect, but basin plans based on one do not transfer to the other. Floor-area-inferred power carries approximately +/-60% uncertainty, calibrated by leave-one-out testing (90% coverage 0.86), while operator-disclosed load is unavailable for all 243 buildings. Finally, temporal resolution changes apparent stress: the draw in the basin containing most buildings is equivalent to 4% of mean annual flow but 23-34% of July flow. Notably, the regional water authority's own assessment of this basin covers on-site use only.
