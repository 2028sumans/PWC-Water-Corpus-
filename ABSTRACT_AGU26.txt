Data-center growth is increasing water demand in already stressed basins, yet facility-level evidence remains limited because operators rarely disclose water use or electricity demand. We examine how accounting choices shape the apparent water burden of data centers by reconstructing the consumptive water footprint of 54 completed data-center buildings in Prince William County, Virginia, using public records, adapting a carbon-accounting scope framework to distinguish on-site cooling, electricity generation, and supply-chain water use. Electricity generation accounts for 88% of the estimated footprint, indicating that most associated water use occurs away from the facilities themselves. This geographic separation makes the choice of accounting method consequential. Average accounting spreads the water used by all generation across all consumers; on that basis more than 40% of the electricity-related water falls on Lake Anna, 80 km away in the York basin rather than the host Potomac. Short-run marginal accounting instead asks which generators respond to added demand, using PJM marginal fuel shares; on that basis Lake Anna receives 0%. Whether a given watershed is implicated therefore depends on the accounting approach, meaning that methodological choices influence which communities, ecosystems, and resources enter the scope of impact assessment and decision-making. Missing information also affects confidence, with power inferred from floor area carrying about ±60% uncertainty, and no operator disclosing the load data needed to resolve it. The regional water authority's own basin assessment is explicitly limited to on-site use, which we estimate at 2.4% of the total footprint; the indirect remainder it flags as out of scope is what we quantify here. Water-management and permitting frameworks therefore need to reach beyond on-site use to the electricity supply chain, and to fix the accounting convention explicitly, since two equally standard conventions disagree about which watersheds are implicated. Which basin is held responsible is currently set by convention rather than by measurement.
