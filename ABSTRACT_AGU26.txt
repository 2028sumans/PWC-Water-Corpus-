Data-center growth is concentrating water demand in stressed basins, yet facility-level evidence is scarce and operators rarely disclose consumption. Recent work maps where these burdens fall nationally, but under a single accounting convention. We ask a prior question: how much of the apparent burden is set by the accounting choice itself?

We build a facility-level Scope 1/2/3 water estimator for 243 data-center buildings in Prince William County, Virginia - within the world's largest data-center region - from public records only: air permits, utility filings, transmission plans, and county GIS. Of these, 54 operate today (10.5 MGD); the rest are permitted or under construction, giving a full-buildout central estimate of 49.6 MGD. Power, the model's root, follows an evidence ladder from permit-documented generator capacity to a floor-area regression, leave-one-out calibrated per building (90% coverage 0.86). We test the distribution against the only metered benchmark available - utility-reported 2023 data compiled by Virginia's legislative audit commission - which covers the study county and is therefore partially, not fully, independent: all six published constraints hold and the shapes are indistinguishable (KS p = 0.09).

First, indirect water dominates (87%), and its basin attribution is convention-dependent: over 40% of electricity-related water is assigned to one reservoir 80 km outside the host basin under location-based accounting, and zero under marginal dispatch, using PJM's published marginal-resource data - a contrast invariant to buildout. Second, inference is costly: floor-area-inferred power carries calibrated +/-60% per-building intervals, while the tier that would resolve it - operator-disclosed load - is empty across all 243 buildings. Third, resolution governs apparent stress: the concentration basin reads 4% of mean annual flow but 23-34% of July flow. The regional authority's own assessment covers on-site use only - 4% of the total.
