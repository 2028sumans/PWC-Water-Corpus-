Data-center growth is increasing water demand in already stressed basins, yet facility-level evidence remains limited because operators rarely disclose water use or electricity demand. We examine how accounting choices shape the apparent water burden of data centers by reconstructing the consumptive water footprint of 54 completed data-center buildings in Prince William County, Virginia, using public records, adapting a carbon-accounting scope framework to distinguish on-site cooling, electricity generation, and supply-chain water use. Electricity generation accounts for 88% of the estimated footprint, indicating that most associated water use occurs away from the facilities themselves. This geographic separation makes the choice of accounting method consequential. Under average accounting, which distributes water used by all electricity generation across electricity consumers, more than 40% of electricity-related water use is assigned to Lake Anna, 80 km away in the York basin rather than the host Potomac. Under short-run marginal accounting, by contrast, which estimates the generators responding to incremental demand using PJM marginal fuel shares, 0% of electricity-related water use is attributed to Lake Anna. Whether a given watershed is implicated therefore depends on the accounting approach, meaning that methodological choices influence which communities, ecosystems, and resources enter the scope of impact assessment and decision-making. Missing information also affects confidence, with power inferred from floor area carrying about ±60% uncertainty, and no operator disclosing the load data needed to resolve it. The regional water authority's own basin assessment is explicitly limited to on-site use, which we estimate at 2.4% of the total footprint; the indirect remainder it flags as out of scope is what we quantify here. Together, these results suggest that data-center water assessment should extend beyond facility-level water use to incorporate electricity-related water use and transparent load data, so that water-management and permitting decisions reflect the full geographic footprint of digital infrastructure.
