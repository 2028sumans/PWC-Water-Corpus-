Data-center growth is increasing water demand in already stressed basins, yet facility-level evidence remains limited because operators rarely disclose water use or electricity demand. We examine how accounting choices shape the apparent water burden of data centers by reconstructing the consumptive water footprint of 54 operating data-center buildings in Prince William County, Virginia, using public records, adapting a carbon-accounting scope framework to distinguish on-site cooling, electricity generation, and supply-chain water use. Electricity generation accounts for 88% of the estimated footprint, indicating that most associated water use occurs away from the facilities themselves. We find that the accounting method determines where this burden appears. Under average accounting, which distributes the water used by all electricity generation across electricity consumers, more than 40% of electricity-related water use is assigned to Lake Anna, 80 km outside the host basin. Under short-run marginal (dispatch) accounting, by contrast, which asks which power plants would ramp to serve new data-center demand, 0% is attributed to Lake Anna. Each accounting approach therefore identifies different watersheds as bearing the indirect water burden, meaning that methodological choices influence which communities, ecosystems, and resources enter the scope of impact assessment and decision-making. We also quantify how missing information affects confidence in these estimates. Power demand inferred from building floor area carries approximately ±60% uncertainty, while no operator publicly discloses the load data needed to resolve this uncertainty. Notably, the regional water authority's own assessment of this basin addresses on-site use only, under one-twentieth of the footprint we estimate. Together, these results show that environmental accounting choices and inference methods can determine not just the magnitude of a data-center water footprint, but where that footprint is geographically assigned and which communities are considered responsible for managing it.
