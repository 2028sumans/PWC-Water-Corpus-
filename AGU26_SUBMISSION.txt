=== TITLE (156 chars incl. spaces / 22 words) ===

Accounting conventions can shift the apparent water burden of data centers across watersheds: a verifiable evidence base for Prince William County, Virginia


=== ABSTRACT (1760/2000 chars, excl. spaces) ===
[Paste this block ONLY into the abstract-body field. Do not include the title.]

Data-center growth is increasing water demand in already stressed basins, yet facility-level evidence remains limited because operators rarely disclose water use or electricity demand. We examine how accounting choices shape the apparent water burden of data centers by reconstructing the consumptive water footprint of 54 completed data-center buildings in Prince William County, Virginia, using public records, adapting a carbon-accounting scope framework to distinguish on-site cooling, electricity generation, and supply-chain water use. Electricity generation accounts for 88% of the estimated footprint, indicating that most associated water use occurs away from the facilities themselves. This geographic separation makes the choice of accounting method consequential. Under average accounting, which distributes water used by all electricity generation across electricity consumers, more than 40% of electricity-related water use is assigned to Lake Anna, 80 km outside the host basin. Under short-run marginal accounting, by contrast, which estimates the generators responding to incremental demand using PJM marginal fuel shares, 0% of electricity-related water use is attributed to Lake Anna. Whether a given watershed is implicated therefore depends on the accounting approach, meaning that methodological choices influence which communities, ecosystems, and resources enter the scope of impact assessment and decision-making. Missing information also affects confidence, with power inferred from floor area carrying about ±60% uncertainty, and no operator disclosing the load data needed to resolve it. Notably, the regional water authority's own assessment of this basin covers on-site use only, under 3% of our estimate. Together, these results suggest that data-center water assessment should extend beyond facility-level water use to incorporate electricity-related water use and transparent load data, so that water-management and permitting decisions reflect the full geographic footprint of digital infrastructure.

=== PLAIN-LANGUAGE SUMMARY (188/200 words) ===

Data centers are expanding rapidly in regions where water is already stressed, yet facility-level water use is almost never reported publicly. We reconstructed the water footprint of 243 data-center buildings in Prince William County, Virginia, from public records alone. Fifty-four operate today, consuming an estimated 10 million gallons per day; if everything approved is built, that could reach 50 million. Most of that water is never used at the buildings; about 88% is consumed at the power plants generating their electricity, often in another river basin. Where this burden appears depends on how it is counted. One standard method assigns more than 40% to Lake Anna, a reservoir 50 miles away. A second established counting approach, which asks which plants ramp up to serve a new data center, assigns Lake Anna none. Neither method is wrong, but each implicates different rivers, communities, and regulators. No operator publishes the electricity data that would narrow these estimates, and the regional water authority's assessment covers only water used at the buildings. Accounting choices thus influence which watershed is treated as responsible, what policies must be made, and which communities must respond.
