=== TITLE (156 chars incl. spaces / 22 words) ===

Accounting conventions can shift the apparent water burden of data centers
across watersheds: a verifiable evidence base for Prince William County, Virginia


=== ABSTRACT (1978/2000 chars, excl. spaces) ===
[Paste this block ONLY into the abstract-body field. Do not include the title.]

Data-center growth is increasing water demand in already stressed basins, yet facility-level evidence remains limited because operators rarely disclose water use or electricity demand. We ask how much the apparent water burden of data centers depends on accounting choices rather than measurement alone. Using public records—air permits, utility filings, transmission plans, and county GIS—we reconstruct the consumptive water footprint of 243 data-center buildings in Prince William County, Virginia, adapting the carbon-accounting scope framework to water by separating on-site cooling, electricity generation, and supply-chain water use. The 54 buildings operating today consume an estimated 10 million gallons per day (MGD), and full buildout would reach roughly 50 MGD. Because 88% of the footprint is associated with electricity generation, most of this water use occurs away from the facilities themselves.

This geographic separation makes the choice of accounting method consequential. Under average accounting, which distributes water used by all generation across all consumers, more than 40% of electricity-related water use is assigned to Lake Anna, 80 km outside the host basin. Under marginal accounting, by contrast, which asks which plants would increase generation to serve new data-center demand, 0% is attributed to Lake Anna. Neither approach is incorrect, but they identify different watersheds—and therefore different downstream communities—as bearing the indirect burden.

Power demand inferred from floor area carries approximately ±60% uncertainty, and no operator discloses the load data needed to resolve it. Nevertheless, per-building estimates fall within 1.3× at every quartile of the intensity-rescaled 2023 utility-reported distribution compiled by Virginia's legislative audit commission. Annual averages likewise obscure local stress: the basin containing most buildings sees a draw equivalent to 3% of mean annual flow but 17–25% of July flow. Notably, the regional water authority's own assessment of this basin addresses on-site use only, under 3% of our estimate. Accounting and inference choices therefore influence not only the magnitude of a data-center water footprint, but where it is geographically assigned and which communities are identified as bearing it.

=== PLAIN-LANGUAGE SUMMARY (198/200 words) ===

Data centers are expanding rapidly in regions where water is already stressed, yet facility-level water use is almost never reported publicly. We reconstructed the water footprint of 243 data-center buildings in Prince William County, Virginia, from public records alone. Fifty-four operate today, consuming an estimated 10 million gallons per day; if everything approved is built, that could reach 50 million. Our building-by-building estimates fall within about 30% of water-use data Virginia's legislature collected from utilities in 2023.

Most of that water is never used at the buildings; about 88% is consumed at the power plants generating their electricity, often in another river basin. Where this burden appears depends on how it is counted. One standard method assigns more than 40% to Lake Anna, a reservoir 50 miles away. A second, equally standard method, which asks which plants ramp up to serve a new data center, assigns Lake Anna none.

Neither method is wrong, but each implicates different rivers, communities, and regulators. No operator publishes the electricity data that would narrow these estimates, and the regional water authority's assessment covers only water used at the buildings. Which watershed is treated as responsible depends on an accounting choice, not measurement.
