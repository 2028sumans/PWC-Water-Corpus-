=== TITLE (156 chars incl. spaces / 22 words) ===

Accounting conventions can shift the apparent water burden of data centers across watersheds: a verifiable evidence base for Prince William County, Virginia


=== ABSTRACT (1990/2000 chars, excl. spaces) ===
[Paste this block ONLY into the abstract-body field. Do not include the title.]

Data-center growth is increasing water demand in already stressed basins, yet facility-level evidence remains limited because operators rarely disclose water use or electricity demand. We examine how accounting choices shape the apparent water burden of data centers by using public records to reconstruct the consumptive water footprint of 54 completed data-center buildings in Prince William County, Virginia, adapting a carbon-accounting scope framework to distinguish on-site cooling (Scope 1), electricity generation (Scope 2), and supply-chain water use (Scope 3). Scope 2 accounts for 88% of the 54 buildings' combined footprint, indicating that most associated water use occurs away from the facilities themselves; the regional water authority's own assessment of the host Potomac basin is nonetheless limited to Scope 1, under 3% of our estimate. This geographic separation makes the accounting method consequential. Under average accounting, which distributes water used by all electricity generation across electricity consumers, more than 40% of electricity-related water use is assigned to Lake Anna, which lies 80 km away in the York basin rather than the host Potomac. Under short-run marginal accounting, by contrast, which estimates the generators responding to incremental demand using PJM marginal fuel shares, under 2% of electricity-related water use is attributed to Lake Anna. Whether a given watershed is implicated therefore depends on the accounting approach, and with it which communities and regulators enter the scope of assessment. Power inferred from floor area carries roughly ±60% uncertainty, and no operator discloses the load data needed to resolve it. Because every scope scales with the same power estimate, this uncertainty cancels in the shares reported here but not in the absolute volumes, leaving the magnitude of any basin's electricity-derived water burden poorly constrained until load data is disclosed. Water-management frameworks must therefore reach beyond on-site use to the electricity supply chain. Carbon accounting already requires dual location- and market-based reporting; water has no equivalent norm, and here the divergence is geographic, moving which basin, and which regulator, is implicated. Which basin is held responsible is set by convention, not measurement.

=== PLAIN-LANGUAGE SUMMARY (197/200 words) ===

Data centers are expanding rapidly in regions where water is already stressed, yet facility-level water use is almost never reported publicly. We estimated the water consumed by 243 data-center buildings in Prince William County, Virginia, from public records alone. Fifty-four have been built. Because server halls are fitted out after a building opens, these draw about 7 million gallons per day today, rising toward 45 million at full buildout. Most of that water is never used at the buildings; about 88% is consumed at the power plants generating their electricity, often in another river basin. Where this burden appears depends on how it is counted. One standard method assigns more than 40% to Lake Anna, a reservoir 50 miles away. A second, which asks which plants respond in the short run to new demand, assigns it about one percent. Neither is wrong, but they disagree about which rivers, communities, and regulators are implicated. No operator publishes the electricity data that would narrow these estimates, and the regional water authority's assessment covers only water used at the buildings. Accounting choices thus influence which watershed is treated as responsible, what policies must be made, and which communities must respond.
