=== ABSTRACT (1987/2000 chars, no spaces) ===

Data-center growth is increasing water demand in already stressed basins, yet facility-level evidence remains limited because operators rarely disclose water use or electricity demand. We ask how much the apparent water burden depends on accounting choices rather than measurement alone. We reconstruct the water footprint of 243 data-center buildings in Prince William County, Virginia, using public records including air permits, utility filings, transmission plans, and county GIS. We adapt the carbon-accounting scope framework to water by separating on-site cooling, electricity generation, and supply-chain water use. Fifty-four buildings currently operate, with an estimated footprint of 10 million gallons per day (MGD), while full buildout could reach roughly 50 MGD. Electricity generation accounts for 87%, meaning most associated water use occurs away from the facilities themselves.

We find that the accounting method determines where this burden appears. Under average accounting, which distributes the water used by all electricity generation across electricity consumers, more than 40% of electricity-related water use is assigned to Lake Anna, 80 km outside the host basin. Under marginal accounting, which asks which power plants would increase generation in response to new data-center demand, Lake Anna receives no additional attribution. Neither approach is inherently incorrect, but they answer different questions and identify different watersheds for water planning.

Missing information also affects confidence: power demand inferred from building floor area carries approximately +/-60% uncertainty, while no operator publicly discloses the load data needed to resolve it. Annual averages likewise obscure local stress, with the basin containing most buildings experiencing a draw equivalent to 4% of mean annual flow but 23-34% of July flow. Results are consistent with utility-reported 2023 data compiled by Virginia's legislative audit commission (KS p = 0.09, n = 54). Notably, the regional water authority's own assessment of this basin addresses on-site use only, under one-twentieth of the footprint we estimate. These results show that accounting choices determine not only the magnitude of a data-center water footprint but where it is assigned and who is responsible for managing it.

=== PLAIN-LANGUAGE SUMMARY (200/200 words) ===

Data centers use a lot of water, but companies rarely disclose how much. We reconstructed the water footprint of 243 data-center buildings in Prince William County, Virginia, using only public records, estimating both the water used at the facilities and the water used by the power plants supplying them.

Most of it is not at the buildings: about 87% is tied to electricity generation. We found that how this water is counted changes where the burden appears. One method assigns much of it to Lake Anna, a reservoir about 50 miles away in another river basin. A second method, which asks which power plants would actually increase production to serve a new data center, assigns Lake Anna none. Both are valid, but they point to different rivers, and therefore different communities and regulators.

Missing information adds uncertainty: without operators disclosing their electricity use, some estimates carry about 60% uncertainty. Yearly averages also hide seasonal stress, since cooling demand peaks when rivers run lowest. Our estimates are consistent with utility-reported data from Virginia's legislative audit commission. Notably, the regional water authority's own assessment of this basin counts only water used at the facilities themselves, a small fraction of the total footprint.
