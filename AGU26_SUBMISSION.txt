=== TITLE (156 chars incl. spaces / 22 words) ===

Accounting conventions can shift the apparent water burden of data centers
across watersheds: a verifiable evidence base for Prince William County, Virginia


=== ABSTRACT (1988/2000 chars, excl. spaces) ===
[Paste this block ONLY into the abstract-body field. Do not include the title.]

Data-center growth is increasing water demand in already stressed basins, yet facility-level evidence remains limited because operators rarely disclose water use or electricity demand. We ask how much the apparent water burden of data centers depends on accounting choices rather than measurement alone. We reconstruct the consumptive water footprint — water lost, not withdrawn — of 243 data-center buildings in Prince William County, Virginia, from public records: air permits, utility filings, transmission plans, and county GIS. We adapt the carbon-accounting scope framework to water by separating on-site cooling, electricity generation, and supply-chain water use. Fifty-four buildings operate today, consuming an estimated 10 million gallons per day (MGD); full buildout could reach roughly 50 MGD. Electricity generation accounts for 88%, meaning most associated water use occurs away from the facilities themselves.

We find that the accounting method determines where this burden appears. Under average accounting, which distributes the water used by all electricity generation across electricity consumers, more than 40% of electricity-related water use is assigned to Lake Anna, 80 km outside the host basin. Under marginal accounting, which asks which power plants would increase generation in response to new data-center demand, Lake Anna receives no additional attribution. Neither approach is incorrect, but they identify different watersheds, and different downstream communities, as bearing the burden.

Missing information also affects confidence: power inferred from floor area carries about ±60% uncertainty, and no operator discloses the load data needed to resolve it. Annual averages likewise obscure local stress, with the basin containing most buildings experiencing a draw equivalent to 3% of mean annual flow but 17–25% of July flow. Per-building estimates fall within 1.3x at every quartile of the intensity-rescaled 2023 utility-reported distribution compiled by Virginia's legislative audit commission (n = 54). Notably, the regional water authority's own assessment of this basin covers on-site use only, under 3% of our estimate. Accounting and inference choices therefore determine not just the magnitude of a data-center water footprint, but where it is assigned and who must manage it.

=== PLAIN-LANGUAGE SUMMARY (198/200 words) ===

Data centers are expanding rapidly in regions where water is already stressed, yet facility-level water use is almost never reported publicly. We reconstructed the water footprint of 243 data-center buildings in Prince William County, Virginia, from public records alone. Fifty-four operate today, consuming an estimated 10 million gallons per day; if everything approved is built, that could reach 50 million. Our building-by-building estimates fall within about 30% of water-use data Virginia's legislature collected from utilities in 2023.

Most of that water is never used at the buildings; about 88% is consumed at the power plants generating their electricity, often in another river basin. Where this burden appears depends on how it is counted. One standard method assigns more than 40% to Lake Anna, a reservoir 50 miles away. A second, equally standard method, which asks which plants ramp up to serve a new data center, assigns Lake Anna none.

Neither method is wrong, but each implicates different rivers, communities, and regulators. No operator publishes the electricity data that would narrow these estimates, and the regional water authority's assessment covers only water used at the buildings. Which watershed is treated as responsible depends on an accounting choice, not measurement.
