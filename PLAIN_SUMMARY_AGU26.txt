Data centers need a lot of water, but we do not know exactly how much water they use because companies rarely release this information. We studied 243 data-center buildings in Prince William County, Virginia, using only public records such as permits, utility documents, and county data. We estimated both the water used at the facilities and the water used by power plants that generate their electricity.

We found that most of the water associated with data centers does not come from the buildings themselves. About 87% is linked to electricity generation. We also found that the way this water is counted changes where the burden appears. One method assigns much of the electricity-related water use to Lake Anna, a reservoir about 50 miles away in another river basin. Another method looks at which power plants would actually increase production because of new data-center demand and does not assign additional water use to Lake Anna. Both methods answer different questions, but they lead to different conclusions about which places are affected, which will affect local water management decisions.

We also found that missing information creates uncertainty. Without data-center operators sharing their electricity use, some estimates carry about +/-60% uncertainty. Finally, yearly averages can hide seasonal water stress. A basin that appears to have enough water over the year can experience much greater stress during dry summer months. To check our work, we compared our estimates against the only measured data available, water use reported by utilities and compiled by Virginia's legislative audit commission, and found them consistent. Notably, the regional water authority's own assessment of this basin looks only at water used at the facilities themselves, which is under a twentieth of the total footprint we estimate. These results show that the way we measure data-center water use affects not only the size of the estimated footprint, but also where that footprint appears and which communities are expected to address it.
